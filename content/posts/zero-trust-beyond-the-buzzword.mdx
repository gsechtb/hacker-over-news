"Zero trust" has been on security vendor slide decks for long enough that it's easy to tune out. That's unfortunate, because underneath the marketing is a genuinely useful architectural shift — one that addresses a specific, well-documented failure mode: the flat internal network.

## The problem zero trust actually solves

The traditional model is a castle: a hardened perimeter (firewall, VPN) around a soft, trusting interior. Once you're on the corporate network — via VPN, via office wifi, via a compromised laptop — you're implicitly trusted by most internal services. Internal traffic is treated as safe traffic.

The problem with that model isn't theoretical. Every ransomware case study in our [anatomy of a ransomware attack](/blog/anatomy-of-a-ransomware-attack) piece has a lateral movement stage, and lateral movement is *only* straightforward because the internal network grants broad implicit trust once you're inside it. A single phished laptop becomes a path to the domain controller because nothing between "inside the VPN" and "domain admin" required re-proving who you were.

Zero trust's core claim, stripped of marketing: **network location should never be a factor in an access decision.** Being on the VPN or in the office proves nothing. Every request — whether it crosses the internet or just crosses a rack in the same data center — gets evaluated on its own merits.

## The three things zero trust actually requires

**1. Strong identity for every request, not just at login.** This means short-lived, cryptographically verifiable tokens (mTLS between services, OIDC tokens for users) attached to each request, rather than "you're on the network, so you're trusted for the rest of the session." Service-to-service calls need identity too — a compromised internal service shouldn't be able to call any other internal service just because they share a subnet.

**2. Policy evaluated per-request, based on more than identity alone.** Device posture (is this laptop's disk encrypted, is its OS patched, is EDR running), location/network risk signals, and the sensitivity of the resource being requested all factor into the decision. This is what conditional access engines (Entra ID Conditional Access, Okta's policies, Google's BeyondCorp-style stack) actually implement — "allow if: valid token AND managed device AND not an anomalous login location AND MFA was phishing-resistant."

**3. Microsegmentation, so a breach in one place doesn't imply access everywhere.** Instead of one flat internal network, workloads are segmented so that, say, the marketing website's backend cannot directly reach the HR database's network segment, regardless of whether both happen to live in the same VPC. This is the direct architectural answer to the lateral-movement stage of a ransomware intrusion — it turns "domain admin from any single compromised host" into "domain admin requires compromising several independently-defended segments."

## What it is not

It's not a single product you install. Any vendor selling "zero trust in a box" is selling one component (usually an identity-aware proxy or a ZTNA replacement for VPN) of what is actually an architectural property spanning identity, device management, network segmentation, and application-level authorization. Buying a ZTNA product without doing the segmentation and policy work gets you a nicer VPN, not zero trust.

It's also not "trust nothing, verify everything, forever" in a way that makes systems unusable. The practical version is risk-adaptive: a low-sensitivity internal wiki might need less friction than a production database admin panel. Zero trust done well is *more* usable for low-risk actions (no VPN needed to check email from a managed laptop) and *more* strict for high-risk ones, not uniformly strict everywhere.

## A realistic adoption path

Most organizations can't flip a switch and segment everything simultaneously — and shouldn't try. A workable sequence:

1. **Inventory identity first.** You can't make per-request decisions without strong identity for users *and* services. SSO for users, workload identity (SPIFFE/SPIRE, cloud-native service identities) for services.
2. **Replace VPN-based trust with an identity-aware proxy for your highest-value internal applications** — the ones that would hurt most if reached by a lateral-movement attacker. Don't try to migrate everything at once.
3. **Segment the network around your crown jewels specifically** — backup infrastructure, domain controllers, databases holding regulated data — before attempting a full microsegmentation rollout. This is the highest-leverage segmentation you can do, and it directly undercuts the ransomware playbook.
4. **Add device posture checks to conditional access policies** once identity and basic segmentation are in place, so "whose laptop is this, and is it healthy" becomes part of every access decision, not just "did they type the right password."
5. **Measure by asking "what can a single compromised laptop reach?"** repeatedly, and shrink the answer over time. That question, asked honestly, is a better zero-trust maturity metric than any vendor's scorecard.

## The honest summary

Zero trust is the architectural response to a specific, repeatedly-exploited failure mode: flat networks that treat "inside the perimeter" as synonymous with "trustworthy." It's a multi-year program, not a purchase, and it's worth pursuing exactly because the alternative — implicit trust based on network location — is the single assumption ransomware operators rely on most.
